How to actually do Lard Yai, Phuket's Sunday night market

5 min read Food & drink Phuket Old Town, Thailand

Bangla Road gets the reputation as Phuket's big night out, and it earns it — bass-heavy bars, neon, a street that never really quiets down. It's also not where you go if you actually want to eat well. That's twenty minutes inland, every Sunday evening, when Thalang Road closes to traffic and Phuket Old Town turns into Lard Yai.

What the street actually looks like

Lard Yai runs the length of Thalang Road and its side lanes, past the same faded Sino-Portuguese shophouses that make the old town worth wandering in daylight. Once the sun goes down, the shopfronts stay lit, folding tables and portable woks fill in the gaps between them, and a stage near one end usually has live music going — less a market than the old town's whole population turning out for the evening.

It's busy, but it's a local kind of busy: families, students, vendors who know half the crowd by name. Tourists show up too, but they're not who the market is for, which is exactly why the food hasn't been dumbed down for them.

What to actually order

Start with mee hokkien, Phuket's own take on stir-fried noodles — thicker and richer than the versions you'll find elsewhere in Thailand, a legacy of the island's Hokkien Chinese tin-mining history. Follow it with o-tao, an oyster omelet fried until the edges crisp, and if you see moo hong on a menu board, order it — a slow-braised pork belly stewed in soy and garlic until it falls apart, another old-town specialty you won't find on a beach-club menu.

Leave room for a-pong, a coconut pancake cooked in a dimpled pan over coals until the edges crackle. It's the kind of dessert that only shows up at markets like this one, made in small batches by vendors who've been running the same stall for years.

Timing it right

Lard Yai runs Sunday evenings only, roughly late afternoon into the night, and it rewards showing up on the earlier side — before six, the street is still filling in and the best stalls haven't sold out of their signature dish yet. By eight, it's at its most crowded, which has its own energy but means longer waits at the stalls worth waiting for.

Worth knowing before you go

Bring cash in small bills — almost nothing here runs on cards, and vendors moving fast through a dinner rush don't want to make change for a large note. There's no seating built into the market itself; people eat standing, perched on a shophouse step, or carry plates to the tables outside the bars ringing the edges. And it's an outdoor, walking market with no real shelter, so a night of rain will thin it out fast — check the forecast before building your evening around it.
